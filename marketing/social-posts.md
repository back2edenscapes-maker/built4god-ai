## 1 — One buyer, one task

“An AI tool for everyone” is hard to investigate. Try: I help [specific buyer] finish [repeated task] without [observable difficulty].

Example hypothesis: help a freelance developer check a client CSV before import. Next, ask someone who recently did that task to walk through what happened. A precise sentence makes an idea easier to test. It does not make demand real.

## 2 — Ask about the last time

Before “Would you use this?”, ask: When did you last do this task? What steps did you take? Where did it slow down? What have you already tried?

Record what happened separately from what you hope will happen. A compliment about your idea is encouraging but weak evidence of willingness to pay. Get permission before recording a conversation and avoid storing sensitive details.

## 3 — The competitor may be a spreadsheet

Your alternatives list should include the current workaround. That might be a spreadsheet, a manual check, or tolerating the problem.

Compare one task: what does each approach require, what does it cost, and what friction remains? Add a date and source. If an existing free option solves it well, narrowing or stopping may be the useful decision.

## 4 — Scope the sample

Write three checks someone else can observe. For an illustrative CSV checker: open a sample locally, identify inconsistent row lengths, and explain the problem in readable language.

Those are proposed acceptance criteria, not software we ship here. A small sample lets prospective users react to something concrete. Test empty and invalid inputs too.

## 5 — A checkout click is a question

A visit, a preview interaction, a checkout click, and a paid order describe different events. Keep them separate.

If people use the preview but do not click checkout, investigate relevance and clarity. If they click but do not buy, check the payment path and ask opt-in users what was unclear. A click is not purchase intent. Include refunds when reviewing money received; leave unknown fees unknown.
