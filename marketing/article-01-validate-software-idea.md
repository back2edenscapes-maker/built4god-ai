# How to validate a small software idea before building it

A software idea becomes easier to evaluate when you can name a buyer, a repeated task, and the difficulty that task creates. You do not need a large survey to start learning. You do need to separate observed behavior from assumptions.

Consider an illustrative idea: a local checker for client CSV files. “Developers need better data tools” is too broad to investigate. “A freelance developer checks a client CSV before importing it” names a situation you can ask about. This example is hypothetical; it is not a report of customer research.

## Start with a task that recently happened

Write one sentence: “I help [buyer] complete [task] without [difficulty].” List what is still unknown. For the CSV example, unknowns include how often files fail, how developers check them now, and whether that approach is satisfactory.

Find an appropriate research setting: an existing relationship, a person who opted in, or a community that explicitly permits research. Do not disguise a sales pitch as a survey. State what you are investigating and let the person decline without repeated follow-up.

## Ask for a walkthrough before showing a solution

Ask about the most recent occurrence. What triggered the task? Which tools were open? What happened next? Where did the work slow down? What was tried already?

Specific details can reveal that you misunderstood the workflow. If a developer says errors are rare and an existing tool catches them, that matters. Record the answer rather than steering the conversation toward your planned product.

Use anonymous identifiers in notes. Keep personal details to the minimum needed and get permission before recording. An interview is a research activity, not permission to add someone to a marketing list.

## Compare the existing workaround

Competition includes manual work, spreadsheets, free tools, and doing nothing. Record each alternative, its task, price where relevant, date checked, and remaining friction.

A proposed gap might be: “The current checker requires uploading a confidential file; this version would run locally.” That is a hypothesis. Perhaps the buyer already has a local tool or the upload is not a problem. Avoid declaring a market underserved before investigating it.

## Make the first sample observable

Choose three acceptance criteria. The hypothetical checker might load an example file, identify inconsistent column counts, and explain the error without uploading client data. These describe something a prospective user can try.

Test normal, empty, and invalid input. Document limits, including file size and unsupported cases. A sample should show the core task clearly enough that feedback concerns behavior rather than imagined features.

## Choose a checkpoint before collecting answers

A starting checkpoint could be five relevant conversations, with three independent descriptions of the same recurring difficulty. This is an operating rule, not statistical proof. Set a checkpoint before reviewing results and explain why it fits the niche.

Decide what evidence would justify a prototype, a narrower offer, or stopping. Compliments are weak evidence of willingness to pay. A real purchase is stronger, but one purchase still does not establish a large market.

## Keep a decision record

Connect the buyer, evidence, alternatives, proposed sample, unanswered questions, and next step. That keeps an exciting idea from turning into a vague commitment to months of work.
